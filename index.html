SautiFlow

SautiFlow Labs · Founding engineer · 2022 - Present

SautiFlow turns a written script into a finished skit: casting, voicing, timing and mix. Creators type the joke, pick a cast and get back ten renderable variations instead of one take they have to record themselves.

Who it is for
  • Solo creators on TikTok, YouTube and WhatsApp who cannot voice five characters in one take.
  • Sketch teams that need a listenable rough cut before booking studio time.
  • Troupes working in Swahili and Sheng, where stock voice packs simply do not exist.
  • Brand and radio teams producing jingle-led spots on a weekly deadline.
How it works
  • Script parser splits dialogue by character and tags emotion, pace and delivery notes.
  • Multi-voice TTS renders three candidate timings per line so the creator picks the take.
  • Voice cloning from 3-5 minutes of licensed reference audio per character.
  • Auto-mix stage adds room, crowd and SFX beds, then normalises to platform loudness targets.
  • Every render is versioned, so re-running a script reproduces the same audio byte for byte.
Results
  • 40+ skits per creator per session, against 3-4 when everything was hand-recorded.
  • Median render of a 45-second, four-character skit: 22 seconds.
  • 2,100 monthly active creators at the last public checkpoint, 61% retained at week four.

PyTorch VITS 2 Whisper FastAPI Celery + Redis React Web Audio API FFmpeg AWS S3 Docker

LabelSet

SautiFlow Labs · Data platform · 2022 - 2024

Most speech models fail on the data, not the architecture. LabelSet is the pipeline behind every model on this page: it sources recordings, gets them labelled by real speakers, and proves the labels are trustworthy before a training run touches them.

Who it is for
  • ML teams that need a labelled corpus with agreement numbers they can defend in review.
  • Research groups collecting under-resourced languages where no public dataset exists.
  • Enterprises labelling internal call recordings that cannot leave their own cloud.
How it works
  • Collection SDK records with device, channel and noise metadata attached from the first second.
  • Each task is routed to three annotators; disagreements go to an adjudicator and back into the guidelines.
  • Gold questions and embedding-based spam detection remove careless work automatically.
  • Pre-labels from Whisper and pyannote mean annotators correct instead of transcribe.
  • Exports land as COCO, WebVTT or Parquet, versioned with a dataset card and a licence per recording.
Results
  • 18,000 hours of audio and 1.2M images delivered across 11 language and accent bands.
  • Inter-annotator agreement reached Cohen's kappa 0.87 after two guideline iterations.
  • Turnaround per batch fell from 3.5 weeks to 9 days; manual labelling time dropped 68%.

EchoID

SautiFlow Labs · Speaker analytics · 2024 - Present

EchoID answers "who said what, and when" across archives nobody has listened to. It turns raw recordings into a speaker-labelled, searchable transcript that legal, research and support teams can actually query.

Who it is for
  • Call centres separating agent and customer turns for QA sampling and compliance.
  • Journalists and researchers working through hundreds of hours of interviews.
  • Legal and compliance teams that must find one sentence in a five-year archive.
  • Meeting-heavy product teams rebuilding action items without re-listening.
How it works
  • VAD segments speech, then pyannote diarises turns and ECAPA-TDNN embeddings cluster them into speakers.
  • Known voices are enrolled once from a short sample, so recurring speakers stay labelled across uploads.
  • ASR adds word-level timestamps, which makes every search result jump straight to the audio.
  • A redaction pass masks phone numbers, IDs and card fragments before anything leaves the pipeline.
  • Everything is indexed in Elasticsearch and exportable as JSON, SRT or Parquet.
Results
  • 89% speaker accuracy on meetings with up to eight speakers; diarisation error rate 12.4%.
  • One hour of four-speaker audio processed in 4 minutes on a single GPU.
  • 40,000 hours indexed for production tenants, with 5 ASR models sharing the same evaluation harness.

Your Swahili ASR demo dies the moment it leaves the laptop

Speech · 18 Sep 2026 · 6 min read

Every team building speech products in this market has the same week: a model scores 6% WER on a public test set, then 34% on recordings from an actual Nairobi street. The gap is almost never the architecture. It is that the evaluation never contained the audio the product will receive.

What actually breaks it
  • Code-switching. A single sentence carrying Swahili, English and Sheng, where the tokeniser has seen two of the three.
  • Channel, not accent. A 40 ms phone downlink with aggressive noise suppression destroys fricatives before the model ever hears them.
  • Distant microphones. One speaker, four people talking, and a laptop three metres away in a tiled room.
  • Numbers, names and money. The exact spans where a wrong transcript costs a customer, and the ones a public test set barely contains.
The loop I run instead
  • Collect a few hours of the product's own audio in week one, before any model decision is made.
  • Score every candidate on that set, sliced by language mix, device and noise band, not one blended average.
  • Wire the harness into CI so a checkpoint that regresses a slice fails the build, not the launch.
  • Fine-tune with LoRA on the domain set: on one Swahili corpus this moved WER from 23.4% to 11.8% without changing the base model.
Takeaways
  • A test set is a product requirement. Write it down before writing the model.
  • Report slices. A mean hides the two cases that lose customers.
  • Better audio beats a bigger checkpoint more often than anyone wants to hear.

A latency budget is a product decision, not a p99

Systems · 07 Aug 2026 · 8 min read

People ask which model gives the lowest latency. The better question is how long a listener will tolerate silence before they think the call has dropped. That number is set by a product owner, and once it exists it decides the architecture for you.

Write the budget down first
  • Pick a first-audio target, not a throughput target. For conversation I start at 500 ms and push down from there.
  • Split it across stages: endpointing, ASR partial, translation, TTS onset, plus a slice of network headroom nobody may spend.
  • Instrument every stage in the same units so a regression names its owner instead of starting a debate.
  • Decide what degrades when the budget is blown: a shorter chunk, a smaller model, or an honest "listening" state in the UI.
Where the milliseconds go
  • Chunking is the biggest lever. Waiting for a clean sentence boundary costs more than the listener will ever forgive.
  • VAD tuning beats model swapping: a hungrier endpointer usually buys 100-150 ms for free.
  • Stream every boundary you can. ASR partials in, tokens out, TTS audio out while generation is still running.
  • Batching on the GPU is a throughput trick that quietly murders p99 if you let it share a queue with live calls.
Takeaways
  • Agree the number before choosing the stack; the number chooses the stack.
  • Ship the budget as a dashboard on day one, or it becomes folklore by month three.
  • On one translation pipeline this discipline held 310 ms median first-audio latency while doubling concurrency.

Labelling audio nobody has labelled before

Data · 21 Jun 2026 · 5 min read

When the language is under-resourced there is no guideline to copy, no gold corpus to fine-tune on and no vendor who can quote you a price. The label quality you get is exactly as good as the process you design around your annotators.

Design for disagreement
  • Write the guideline as decisions, not principles: what happens to a laugh, an overlapping "mm-hm", a code-switched brand name.
  • Route every task to three annotators and adjudicate the disagreements. Their disagreements are the guideline's bug report.
  • Seed gold questions to catch fast workers, and use embedding distance to catch audio that is not what the worker thinks they are labelling.
  • Report agreement as a number. Two guideline iterations took one Sheng call corpus from kappa 0.71 to 0.87.
Make correction cheaper than transcription
  • Pre-label with Whisper and a diariser, then ask annotators to fix rather than type. It cut labelling time 68% on the same budget.
  • Keep speaker and segment boundaries in the schema from day one; retrofitting them means re-listening to everything.
  • Store device, channel and collection context beside the audio, because that is what explains a model's failure six months later.
  • Ship a dataset card with the licence, the collection consent basis and the known gaps.
Takeaways
  • Agreement is the deliverable. A transcribed corpus nobody can defend is a cost, not an asset.
  • Annotators are domain experts paying you for the guideline you refused to write.
  • Batch turnaround fell from 3.5 weeks to 9 days once pre-labelling and adjudication were in the same pipeline.

Brand voices that survive production

Synthesis · 03 May 2026 · 7 min read

A synthetic narrator is a brand asset with a legal life and an operational one. Most teams only ever design for the demo, then discover the voice cannot be licensed for ads, or cannot hold up at 40 concurrent calls. Both are fixable at the recording stage.

Before anyone enters the booth
  • Agree territory, media and expiry in writing. A voice built for an IVR is not automatically a voice for a TV spot.
  • Record 30-60 minutes of read script that matches the domains the product will actually speak: numbers, currencies, names, apologies.
  • Fix the mic chain and the room, then keep the notes. Re-recording at a different distance changes the voice, not just the audio.
  • Get consent for the specific use, and store it next to the model weights so the next engineer finds it.
Then design for the serving path
  • Expose pace, pitch range and emotion as three real controls. A hidden embedding nobody can steer becomes a support ticket.
  • Set a time-to-first-audio target and build to it: 42 concurrent voices fit on one GPU after batching and TensorRT work that a naive server could not.
  • Run a blind listening test against the original speaker before shipping. Mean opinion score of 3.9 / 5 on Kenyan English beat the 3.4 vendor baseline it replaced.
  • Version the weights with the script that produced them, or accept that the brand voice will drift and nobody can say when.
Takeaways
  • Licensing and serving constraints are recording decisions, not deployment decisions.
  • A voice is only finished when it has an owner, an expiry date and a latency number.
